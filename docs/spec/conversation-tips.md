# Conversation tips for "Talk it through"

Short tips for UI cards, grouped by stage. Each tip is 45 words or fewer, written in the second person, and makes no therapy claims. `[S#]` points to the Sources list. **Evidence** notes are for the team and are not meant to be shown in the UI.

> **How the sources were checked:** In this session, direct page fetches were blocked by the network allowlist (gottman.com, thehotline.org, cnvc.org, pubmed and others). Every URL below came back from a live web-search index matching the claimed title or content. I did not open the pages and read them in full. Before shipping, someone should click through them once. URLs marked † come from a WordPress short link or a sibling domain, so the canonical slug should also be confirmed.

---

## Global copy rules (apply to every card)

- Call these **"tips"**, **"ideas"** or **"ways couples often find helpful"**. Never call them treatment, therapy, counselling, a diagnosis or a cure. Don't say "clinically proven".
- Credit sources as "inspired by" or "adapted from" (e.g. "Adapted from Gottman Institute research"). Don't say "Gottman-certified" or use trademarked program names as if the app delivers them. Gottman Method, PREP, Imago, Hold Me Tight and EFT are all branded programs.
- Don't quote headline statistics in the UI (96%, 5:1, 69%, 81%). See Evidence caveats.
- Keep a persistent footer such as: *"Relationship Menu is a conversation tool, not therapy or counselling. If you feel unsafe, [get confidential support]."*
- Every card must keep the Off limits rule intact: nothing in the UI should nudge someone to change an Off limits answer.

## 1. Before you start

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| B1 | Check you both feel safe | These tips assume you both feel safe to speak freely. If you're afraid of how your partner might react, or feel pressured or controlled, skip this conversation. You can talk confidentially with a domestic abuse advocate instead. | Before | [S20] [S21] |
| B2 | Pick a time, not a moment | Choose a calm time you both agree on, not late at night or in passing. Some couples set a regular weekly check-in so hard topics never feel like an ambush. | Before | [S9] |
| B3 | Set a time limit and a focus | Agree how long you'll talk, say 30–45 minutes, and pick one or two items to start. You don't need to cover every difference at once. The menu will still be here next time. | Before | [S9] [S12] |
| B4 | Agree a pause signal | Before you begin, agree on a word or gesture either of you can use to pause, and promise to come back. Knowing you can stop makes it easier to stay. | Before | [S6] [S12] |
| B5 | Find the story behind your answer | Look at your Must haves and Off limits and ask yourself: what hope, value or past experience sits behind this one? Sharing that "why" usually lands better than the answer alone. | Before | [S8] |

**Evidence:**
- B2/B3: Scheduling and time-boxing are practitioner recommendations (the Gottman "State of the Union" meeting, PREP's ground rules). They haven't been tested as standalone techniques, and the 30–45 minute figure is a convention, not a research finding. Gottman's own pages describe the State of the Union meeting as about an hour a week [S9].
- B5: Adapted from Gottman's "dreams within conflict" idea [S8]. That's a clinical concept, not an experimentally tested intervention.

## 2. Opening

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| O1 | Start gently | Open with "I" and something specific rather than "you always…". Try: "I feel ___ about ___, and I'd love ___." A gentle opening makes it easier for your partner to listen instead of defend. | Opening | [S1] [S13] |
| O2 | Begin with common ground | Start with items you both marked Must have or Like to have. Naming what you share first can make the differences feel like something you're facing together. | Opening | [S10] [S2] |
| O3 | Describe, don't diagnose | Point to what's actually there ("You marked this Off limits") rather than a judgment ("You're so closed off"). Neutral, specific words are easier to hear. | Opening | [S17] [S19] |
| O4 | Feeling, need, request | Share a feeling, the need behind it, and one doable request: "I feel lonely when we skip date night. I need closeness. Could we plan one evening a week?" A request leaves room for "no". A demand doesn't. | Opening | [S17] [S18] |
| O5 | Ask clearly for what you want | Describe the situation, say how you feel, ask plainly for what you'd like, and say why it would matter to you. Then stay on topic and be open to a different version of your ask. | Opening | [S19] |
| O6 | Understand first, solve later | Decide together whether this talk is for understanding or for deciding. Many couples find it helps to fully hear each other first and save problem-solving for later. | Opening | [S12] [S11] |

**Evidence:**
- O1: The underlying study is Carrère & Gottman (1999) [S13]. They coded the first 3 minutes of a 15-minute lab conflict discussion for 124 newlywed couples and found it predicted divorce or stability over 6 years (17 divorces). That's correlational divorce prediction. It does not show that a soft opening *causes* a good conversation. Don't put "96% of conversations end the way they start" in the UI (see caveats).
- O2: This is an app design choice *inspired by* Gottman's positive-to-negative ratio findings [S10] [S14]. It is not a named, tested technique.
- O4: NVC [S17]. Its research base is small and mixed (see caveats). The tip uses the four-part structure as a phrasing aid only.
- O5: DBT's DEAR MAN (Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, stay Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate) [S19], paraphrased without the acronym. DBT was developed and tested as a treatment, mainly for emotion dysregulation, not as couples advice. Cite it as "adapted from".
- O6: PREP's rule is to separate problem discussion from problem solving [S12].

## 3. Listening

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| L1 | Take turns holding the floor | Hold an object to show who's speaking. The listener's only job is to sum up what they heard, in their own words, until the speaker says "yes, that's it." Then swap. No solving yet. | While listening | [S11] [S12] |
| L2 | Mirror back | Repeat back what you heard, then ask: "Did I get that? Is there more?" Keep going until your partner feels fully heard. Mirroring isn't agreeing. It shows you were really listening. | While listening | [S15] |
| L3 | Validate without agreeing | Let your partner know their view makes sense from where they stand: "Given what you've told me, I can see why you'd want that." You can validate and still keep your own answer. | While listening | [S15] [S19] |
| L4 | Guess the feeling, then check | Try naming what your partner might feel ("I imagine you might feel nervous about this?") and let them correct you. A guess that's a bit off, followed by a check, still shows you care. | While listening | [S15] [S17] |
| L5 | Find the part you can say yes to | Look for some part of your partner's view you can genuinely accept, even a small one. Letting each other's perspective shape decisions tends to help both people feel respected. | While listening | [S7] |
| L6 | Keep it gentle and interested | Keep your tone gentle, put the phone away, and show interest with eye contact and questions. A little lightness helps too. How you listen matters as much as what you say. | While listening | [S19] |

**Evidence:**
- L1: The Speaker-Listener Technique is the core of PREP [S11] [S12]. PREP as a whole has some RCT support. But a 2014 study found no link between how often couples used the technique and their later satisfaction [S24]. Gottman et al. (1998) [S7] also reported that paraphrase-and-validate listening was rare even in happy couples during conflict and didn't predict outcomes. That led Gottman to question active-listening training. Stanley, Bradbury & Markman (2000) [S25] rebutted this on methodological grounds. The technique remains useful as *structure for a hard talk*. Don't claim it improves relationships.
- L2–L4: Imago Dialogue (mirroring, validation, empathy) [S15]. The only RCT found (Gehlert et al.) reported statistically significant but **not clinically significant** gains in satisfaction [S16]. Present it as a listening structure only.
- L5: "Accepting influence" comes from Gottman et al. (1998) [S7]: 130 heterosexual newlywed couples, where *husbands* accepting *wives'* influence predicted stability. The tip is written gender-neutral, which is a generalisation. The "81% chance of divorce" figure that circulates on gottman.com blog posts has no clear published source. Don't use it.
- L6: DBT's GIVE skill (Gentle, Interested, Validate, Easy manner) [S19], paraphrased.

## 4. When it gets heated

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| H1 | Swap the four conversation-killers | Watch for criticism, contempt (eye-rolls, mockery), defensiveness and shutting down. If you notice one, swap it: a gentle restart, an appreciation, owning your part, or a calming break. | When heated | [S2] |
| H2 | Own a piece of it | Instead of defending, find even a small part you can own: "You're right, I did change the subject." Taking some responsibility often lowers the temperature for both of you. | When heated | [S2] |
| H3 | Offer and accept repairs | A joke, a touch, "Can we start over?" or "I'm getting defensive" are all ways to repair. When your partner offers one, try to take it, even if it's clumsy. | When heated | [S3] [S4] |
| H4 | Take a real break when flooded | If your heart is racing or you can't think straight, say "I'm overwhelmed, I need a break" and agree when you'll come back. Take at least 20 minutes doing something calming, not replaying the argument. | When heated | [S6] |
| H5 | Name the loop, not the villain | If one of you pushes to keep talking and the other pulls away, that's a very common loop, not a character flaw. Try naming it together: "We're in our chase-and-retreat pattern again." | When stuck | [S22] [S27] |
| H6 | Share the softer feeling | Under anger there's often worry, hurt or fear of losing each other. Try saying that softer part out loud: "I get loud because I'm scared this matters less to you." | When stuck | [S22] |
| H7 | Some differences are for keeping | Many differences between partners never fully go away, and that's normal. The goal can be understanding each other and finding a way to live with it, not deciding who's right. | When stuck | [S5] |
| H8 | Get curious about the dream | When you're stuck, swap persuading for curiosity: "What does this mean to you? Is there a story behind it? What would your ideal look like?" Don't try to solve it yet. | When stuck | [S8] |

**Evidence:**
- H1/H2: The Four Horsemen and their antidotes (gentle start-up, appreciation and respect, taking responsibility, physiological self-soothing) are as described by the Gottman Institute [S2]. The horsemen as divorce predictors come from Gottman's observational studies [S14]. Those are correlational, and the prediction accuracy was not cross-validated (see [S26]).
- H3: The repair-attempt definition is "any verbal or non-verbal action that prevents negativity from escalating" (Gottman Research FAQ [S4]). Repair success is described as predictive, but this too is from observational research.
- H4: Gottman guidance [S6] is at least 20 minutes, a return time within 24 hours, and the person who asks for the break initiates the return. The ~100 bpm "flooding" threshold is a rule of thumb, and the "20 minutes for adrenaline to clear" explanation is simplified. **Don't show heart-rate numbers in the UI.** A break with a set return time is also how the app should frame "stepping away", so it isn't confused with stonewalling.
- H5/H6: EFT's "demon dialogues" (Find the Bad Guy, Protest Polka, Freeze and Flee) from Johnson's *Hold Me Tight* [S22]. The pursue/withdraw pattern is independently well documented as "demand/withdraw" (Christensen & Heavey 1990 [S27]). EFT as *therapy* has a reasonable evidence base [S23], but that doesn't transfer to self-help tips. The Hold Me Tight group program has only uncontrolled studies so far [S28].
- H7: Gottman's "69% of conflicts are perpetual" [S5] comes from his lab and clinical observations and is quoted without published methods. The tip deliberately says "many" rather than a number.

## 5. Off limits and boundaries

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| X1 | "No" is a complete answer | An Off limits answer doesn't need a reason or a defense. You can gently ask if they'd like to share more, but it's completely fine if they don't. | Opening / any time | [S29] |
| X2 | Don't negotiate a no | This isn't the place to talk someone out of an Off limits. Repeated asking, bargaining or guilt turns a "yes" into something that isn't freely given. | Any time | [S29] |
| X3 | Answers can change, both ways | Anyone can change an answer at any time, including from yes to no. Saying yes to one thing doesn't mean yes to anything related. | Any time | [S29] |
| X4 | Thank them for their honesty | Hearing a no can sting. Try "Thanks for telling me" before anything else. Honest limits are what make the yeses trustworthy. | While listening | [S29] [S19] |
| X5 | When a Must have meets an Off limits | If something you need is off limits for your partner, that's important to know, not a failure. Talk about what each of you can live with. Some differences need outside support or more time. | When stuck | [S5] |
| X6 | When "no" doesn't feel safe | If saying no to your partner feels scary, or a no has led to threats, punishment or pressure, that's not a communication problem. You can get confidential support any time. | Any time (persistent link) | [S20] [S21] |

**Evidence:**
- X1–X3: Adapted from the consent principles that consent is *freely given*, *reversible* and *specific* (Planned Parenthood's FRIES model [S29]). FRIES was written for sexual consent. Applying it to all Off limits answers is a reasonable values-based design choice, not a research finding, and the copy should present it that way.
- X4: App-original wording, loosely based on validation (DBT GIVE [S19]). There's no specific study behind it.
- X5: Avoid suggesting that the person with the Off limits should move. The tip keeps the focus on what each person "can live with". It deliberately doesn't use the word "dealbreaker", which could pressure people.
- X6: For US users, show the National Domestic Violence Hotline: **1-800-799-7233**, text **START to 88788**, or chat at thehotline.org [S21]. Localise for other countries.

## 6. Closing

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| C1 | Sum up together | Before you finish, each say one thing you heard and any small next step you agreed. "We understand each other better and we'll come back to this" is a fine ending. | Closing | [S12] |
| C2 | End with appreciations | Each name something you appreciated, about the conversation or each other. Specific beats general: "I appreciated you staying with it when it got hard." | Closing | [S9] [S2] |
| C3 | One thing for this week | Ask each other: "What's one small thing I could do this week that would help you feel closer?" Keep it doable. | Closing | [S9] |
| C4 | Leave it unfinished if needed | If you didn't get through everything, that's okay. Agree when you'll pick it up again rather than pushing on while tired. | Closing | [S6] [S9] |

**Evidence:**
- C2/C3: The Gottman State of the Union meeting opens with appreciations and ends with "what can I do to make you feel loved this week" [S9]. Ending with appreciation also matches the antidote to contempt [S2]. This is a practitioner recommendation, not tested on its own.

## 7. Between conversations

| # | Name | Tip (UI copy) | When | Source |
|---|------|---------------|------|--------|
| A1 | Keep up the small positives | Everyday thanks, interest and affection build a cushion for hard talks. Couples who stay together tend to have far more warm moments than negative ones, even when they disagree. | After | [S10] [S14] |
| A2 | Come back to a talk that went badly | If a conversation went wrong, revisit it once you're calm: "Can we talk about how that went?" Share what each of you felt, without re-arguing the topic. | After | [S3] |
| A3 | Check in regularly | A short weekly check-in, starting with what's going well, makes it easier to raise things early before they grow. | After | [S9] |
| A4 | Revisit the menu | Your answers can change as you do. Agree to look at the menu again in a few months, and treat changed answers with curiosity rather than suspicion. | After | [S29] [S5] |
| A5 | Know when to get more support | If the same topic keeps ending in hurt, or one of you dreads these talks, a qualified couples counsellor or a relationship-education course can help. This app isn't a substitute for that. | After | [S12] [S23] [S28] |

**Evidence:**
- A1: Deliberately avoids the "5:1" number (see caveats).
- A4: App-original. It extends the "reversible" consent principle.
- A5: In abusive relationships, point to individual support and advocates, not couples counselling [S20].

## Safety caveats (for the product team)

1. **Everything here assumes a safe, roughly equal relationship.** These are tools for differences between people who both feel free to say no. They aren't meant for situations involving fear, coercion, control or abuse.
2. **Don't steer people in abusive relationships toward joint conversations.** The National Domestic Violence Hotline states: *"We at The Hotline do not encourage anyone in an abusive relationship to seek counseling with their partner."* Its reasons are that abuse isn't a relationship problem, that joint sessions can't fix the unequal power structure, that a facilitator may not know about the abuse, and that what's said can later be used against the partner [S20] [S21]. A guided "Talk it through" page is a lighter version of the same risk. An abusive partner could use answers or "agreements" from it as leverage.
3. **Product implications:**
   - Show a short safety note before the first Talk it through session (B1), and keep a "Feel unsafe?" link on every screen (X6).
   - Never surface one person's private answers to the other without the sharing step both agreed to.
   - Avoid features that let one partner "remind", nudge or count how often the other said no.
   - Consider a quick-exit button on safety pages.
4. **Crisis:** For immediate danger, say "call your local emergency number" (911 in the US). Hotline: 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788 (US).

## Evidence caveats

1. **"96% of conversations end the way they start."** This isn't in the Carrère & Gottman (1999) abstract [S13]. That study is about **predicting divorce over 6 years** from the first 3 minutes of a 15-minute lab conflict discussion (124 newlywed couples, 17 divorces), using coded emotion. The 96% figure comes from Gottman's popular writing and interviews (e.g. as quoted in *TIME*). The study doesn't test what happens to a conversation's outcome if you change how it starts. Safe phrasing: "Research suggests how a disagreement starts is linked to how it goes."
2. **Divorce-prediction accuracy is overstated in general.** Gottman's high accuracy figures come from models fit and tested on the same small samples, without cross-validation. Heyman & Slep (2001) showed that predictive accuracy "drops precipitously" when such models are cross-validated [S26]. Stanley, Bradbury & Markman (2000) criticised over-extending this basic research into intervention advice [S25].
3. **The "5:1 magic ratio".** This is an average observed **during lab conflict discussions** in stable couples (Gottman & Levenson 1992; Gottman 1994) [S14] [S10]. It's a descriptive correlate, not a target to hit or a daily quota. Gottman's own blog adds a 20:1 figure for non-conflict interaction [S10]. Also don't confuse it with the Losada/Fredrickson 2.9 "positivity ratio", whose mathematics was debunked (Brown, Sokal & Friedman 2013, *American Psychologist*, doi:10.1037/a0032850; not re-verified this session).
4. **"69% of problems are perpetual"** and **"81% chance of divorce if men don't accept influence"** are widely repeated on gottman.com blog posts without published methods. The underlying accepting-influence study [S7] was of heterosexual newlyweds and specifically about husbands. Use qualitative wording only.
5. **Active listening and the Speaker-Listener Technique.** These are useful structures for a hard talk, but the evidence that *using them* improves relationships is mixed [S7] [S24] [S25]. PREP overall has stronger support than the single technique.
6. **Imago Dialogue.** The one RCT found reported improvement that was statistically significant but **not clinically significant** [S16]. The evidence is thin.
7. **Nonviolent Communication.** The research is small, varied and mostly uses non-standard measures. There are no robust couples RCTs [S18]. Use it as a phrasing aid only.
8. **EFT / Hold Me Tight.** EFT couples *therapy* has a reasonable evidence base (Wiebe & Johnson 2016 review [S23]), though much of it comes from the developers' own group. The *Hold Me Tight* group program has only uncontrolled studies so far, with moderate effects that faded in more distressed couples [S28]. Self-help tips drawn from it are untested.
9. **DBT skills (DEAR MAN, GIVE)** were built for a clinical treatment package. Lifting them out as tips is reasonable but untested for couples. Cite them as "adapted from".
10. **Samples.** Most of this research involved married, heterosexual, mostly white, Western couples. Gottman's work on same-sex couples found broadly similar patterns but is smaller. Nothing here was studied with polyamorous or multi-partner groups, and the app supports "two or more people", so treat those adaptations as untested.
11. **The consent framing** (X1–X3) is a values-based design principle adapted from sexual-consent education. It is not an empirical finding, and the copy shouldn't present it as one.
